# **1. Executive Summary**
India generates an estimated 7.7–7.8 million tonnes of textile waste annually — about 8.5% of the world's total — yet the system that handles it is fragmented, largely informal, and poorly documented. There is no single government or industry registry of textile recycling centers in India. Capacity is split across a small number of named industrial hubs (most notably Panipat, Haryana), a handful of organized NGO and startup collection networks, and a vast, uncounted informal sector of scrap dealers and waste pickers.

This document consolidates the available statistics on collection volume, processing capacity, and material flow; documents two structural problems — fragmentation/opacity in the recycling supply chain, and serious occupational health harm to recycling workers — and maps each problem to a concrete KaPhor feature, extending the gap-analysis already presented in the KaPhor research paper.

# **2. Statistics: Volume, Flow, and Capacity**
## **2.1 How much textile waste India generates**
Estimates vary by source and methodology, but converge in a fairly tight range:

- Fashion for Good's “State of Textile Waste in India” report: 7,793 thousand tonnes/year (8.5% of global textile waste).
- Center for Study of Science, Technology and Policy (CSTEP), cited via Deccan Herald: “around 78 lakh tonnes” (7.8 million tonnes) annually; textile waste is the third-largest contributor to municipal solid waste in India, after plastics.
- Landbell GreenForest Solutions (EPR webinar, 2024): ~7.7 million tonnes/year, ~8.5% of world total.
- Lower-end industry estimates (“best colorful socks” statistics aggregator and others): figures as low as 1.2–5–6 million tonnes/year appear in some secondary sources — these are less rigorously sourced and should be treated with caution relative to the Fashion for Good / CSTEP figures above.

For KaPhor's purposes, the ~7.7–7.8 million tonnes/year figure (Fashion for Good, CSTEP, Landbell) is the best-supported estimate and is recommended as the citation baseline alongside the existing Ellen MacArthur Foundation global figure of 92 million tonnes/year.
## **2.2 Where the waste comes from**
Fashion for Good's breakdown of the ~7.8 million tonnes is the most granular composition data found:

|**Source category**|**Share**|**Notes**|
| :- | :- | :- |
|Domestic post-consumer|51%|Discarded clothing and home textiles from Indian households; hardest to collect and sort|
|Pre-consumer|42%|Fabric cut-offs, yarn scraps, factory offcuts; easiest to recycle (clean, single-material, traceable)|
|Imported post-consumer|7%|Used clothing and mutilated rags imported mainly for recycling/reprocessing, regulated under the Hazardous and Other Wastes (Management and Transboundary Movement) Rules, 2016|

*Table 2. Composition of India's annual textile waste stream. Source: Fashion for Good, “Sorting for Circularity India – Wealth in Waste.”*

A separate Ministry of Textiles statement (in response to May 2026 international reporting on Panipat) reinforced that over 90% of the waste processed nationally originates domestically — important context for any narrative suggesting India is mainly a dumping ground for other countries' textile waste. Imports are a real and consequential flow (see 2.4 and Section 3.2), but they are a minority share nationally, even though they dominate certain hubs like Panipat.
## **2.3 What happens to the waste once generated — the reuse/recycle/landfill split**
This is the closest available proxy for “drop-in volume” at recycling centers, since no center-level intake data is publicly published. Two independently sourced breakdowns were found, and they should be read as complementary estimates rather than reconciled into one number:
### **Fashion for Good breakdown (by waste source)**
- 59% of total waste generated is reused or recycled across all channels (formal + informal).
- Of that reused/recycled share, only 10–40% re-enters global supply chains as new raw material — the rest stays in lower-value reuse (rags, stuffing, low-grade yarn).
- 43% of domestic post-consumer waste ends up in landfill, versus only ~1% of pre-consumer and imported waste (because pre-consumer/imported waste is cleaner, more uniform, and economically worth processing).
- 64% of imported waste specifically is either recycled into yarn within India or exported for reuse (mostly to Africa and Europe after sorting).
### **CSTEP / Down To Earth breakdown (by outcome)**
- 34% of India's textile waste is reused, through repair or conversion into new products.
- 25% is recycled into yarns — the base material for new fabric — though most of this recycled yarn fails to meet global quality standards.
- By implication, roughly 41% is neither reused nor recycled (landfill, incineration, or unaccounted loss).

A third, frequently cited figure from a regulatory-intervention paper states that globally only 12–15% of textile waste reaches recycling facilities at all, and of that, only 1% is recycled into new clothing — this is a global figure, not India-specific, but it illustrates why “recycled” does not mean “closed-loop” in this industry, a distinction directly relevant to KaPhor's eco-impact tracker claims.
## **2.4 Panipat: the single largest data point available**
Panipat, Haryana is the only Indian textile recycling cluster with concrete, repeatedly-cited operating statistics, making it the de facto case study for “what a recycling center drop-in actually looks like” at scale:

|**Metric**|**Figure**|
| :- | :- |
|Annual volume processed|1,000,000+ tonnes of used clothing/year (The Guardian, via Dailyhunt/Deccan Chronicle reporting); “more than half a million tons” in an earlier Atmos estimate — the range reflects different reporting periods/methodologies|
|Number of recycling units|~20,000 units currently operating in Panipat (Guardian/Dailyhunt); separately, ~100 units reported by one industry source — likely reflects different unit-size thresholds (registered larger units vs. all operating units including very small ones)|
|Historical peak (national)|400+ recycling units nationally during the 1990s–2000s boom, since fallen to roughly 100 by one estimate, with industry revenue down from ~USD 300M to ~USD 62M|
|Workers employed|~300,000 (Guardian estimate) to 200,000+ (Atmos); a separate estimate puts India's textile recycling workforce nationally at up to 4 million|
|Material origin|Imported bales from the US, UK, Japan, and other countries, arriving via the ports of Kandla and Mundra|
|Output destinations|Yarn and blankets supplied to IKEA, Walmart, and refugee-aid programs across Africa and the Middle East|
|Trade exposure|A 50% US tariff on Indian textile exports (added atop an earlier levy, reported in 2025–26) has materially reduced demand and margins for Panipat's output|

*Table 3. Panipat textile recycling cluster — operating statistics. Figures vary by source and reporting date; treat as directional rather than precise.*


# **3. Problems**
## **3.1 Problem: Fragmentation and opacity in the recycling supply chain**
There is no unified, centrally maintained directory of textile recycling centers, drop-off points, or processing facilities in India. Capacity is split across at least five distinct, non-overlapping data sources, none of which talk to each other:

1. Industrial recycling clusters (Panipat being the dominant, almost only well-documented example).
1. EPR-registered intermediaries (44,659 plastic intermediaries, 7,050 e-waste entities, and 2,933 battery handlers are registered nationally under Extended Producer Responsibility rules — but textile-specific EPR registration counts were not found in any source reviewed, suggesting textiles lag behind plastics and e-waste in regulatory tracking).
1. NGO and social-enterprise donation/reuse networks (Goonj, Share At Door Step, Bombay Recycling Concern, and similar), which primarily route wearable clothing to resale/redistribution rather than material recycling.
1. A small number of named startups and “Textile Recovery Facilities” (Saahas Zero Waste, ReCircle, Respun, EcoDhaga) that specifically target unwearable textiles for fiber recycling, concentrated in Bengaluru, Gurugram, Delhi-NCR, and Mumbai.
1. The informal sector — kabadiwalas, scrap dealers, and the Waghri/Devipujak community's traditional cloth-for-utensils barter network — which is undocumented in any registry but is widely acknowledged to handle a very large share of actual textile collection nationally.

The practical consequence: a consumer or a platform like KaPhor cannot currently determine, for any given garment, which of these five channels it will actually pass through, what happens to it there, or how much of its mass is genuinely recycled versus downcycled, exported, or landfilled. This is precisely the friction problem that Hagiu and Wright's multi-sided platform theory predicts will suppress both supply and demand — directly relevant to KaPhor's existing Lifecycle Optimisation Engine design rationale.
## **3.2 Problem: Worker health harm in textile recycling, concentrated in import-heavy hubs**
This is independently confirmed across multiple investigative sources (CNN, The Guardian, ThePrint, Atmos, The Diplomat, Ecotextile News) reporting from Panipat between October 2025 and May 2026. The pattern is consistent across all of them:
### **Health outcomes documented**
- Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD) is reported by Panipat Civil Hospital as the most common condition among textile workers, attributed directly to inhaling dust and cloth fibres.
- A health survey found 93% of families living around recycling clusters reported serious health issues — including chronic respiratory disease, tuberculosis, and cancer — within the past five years.
- A respiratory specialist treating affected workers describes a progressive pattern: breathing difficulty that worsens over time and can advance to fibrosis, which is largely irreversible once established.
- Workers in dyeing and bleaching units — disproportionately women, who are concentrated in early-stage sorting and cutting where dust exposure is highest — handle sulphuric acid and dyes without gloves, and report skin infections and respiratory irritation, with no on-site medical facilities.
### **Documented root causes**
- Protective equipment is inconsistently used: workers report removing masks within hours due to heat and discomfort; one factory owner stated masks are provided but not worn, while a labour-department official and outside researchers describe the working environment itself as the underlying driver, not just non-compliance.
- Untreated wastewater from dyeing and bleaching flows through open drains, with reporting indicating it can reach the Yamuna River — an environmental, not just occupational, harm.
- Regulatory enforcement is weak: pollution-control penalties are issued but “rarely enforced,” per government data cited in reporting.
### **The import-volume connection**
Much of the highest-risk material handled in Panipat specifically is imported fast-fashion waste from the US, UK, Japan, and Europe — countries where consumers discard clothing with little visibility into its downstream destination. This is the mechanism behind the claim that other countries' textile waste contributes to harm for underprivileged Indian workers: the harm is real and well-documented, but it is concentrated in specific import-heavy processing hubs (Panipat above all) rather than evenly distributed across India's textile recycling sector as a whole. India's Ministry of Textiles has publicly pushed back on international coverage (notably CNN's May 2026 report) as an overstatement of the import share, while not disputing the underlying worker-health findings.

This distinction matters for KaPhor: the problem is not “textile recycling causes respiratory illness” as an inherent property of the process — it is a specific, addressable failure of protective equipment, ventilation, and enforcement at high-volume, import-heavy, weakly regulated facilities. That framing is what makes a “verified facility” feature meaningful rather than performative.


# **4. Proposed Solutions — Mapped to KaPhor**
The two problems above are not currently addressed by any of KaPhor's ten existing research-gap-to-feature mappings (Table II in the KaPhor paper). The following two additions are proposed in the same format, extending the existing table rather than replacing it.
## **4.1 Gap 11: No circular fashion platform discloses or routes around labour conditions in textile recycling**
None of the 15 sources reviewed in the KaPhor paper address supply-chain labour conditions at the physical recycling/recovery stage. Geissdoerfer et al. and Blackburn et al. cover circular business models and platform research gaps respectively, but neither extends to occupational safety in recycling facilities. This is a genuine, citable gap — evidenced by investigative/practitioner sources (CNN, Guardian, ThePrint, Atmos) rather than peer-reviewed literature, which should be noted explicitly given the source-type shift from the rest of the paper's evidence base.
### **Proposed KaPhor component: Verified Facility Tier**
- Extend the existing Lifecycle Optimisation Engine's RECYCLE-state routing logic with a facility verification flag, rather than building new infrastructure.
- Onboard a starter set of named, addressable partners already identified in this research — Goonj's Processing Centres, Saahas Zero Waste's Textile Recovery Facility, Respun, and EcoDhaga — prioritised because they are traceable organisations with public facing operations, not anonymous bulk-export channels.
- Verification criteria (minimum bar, expandable over time): documented PPE provision, basic ventilation/dust controls, and no open-drain effluent discharge for any wet-processing steps.
- Surface this at the point of disposal choice in the app — e.g. “This item will go to [Verified Partner] for fiber recycling” — rather than silently routing to default/cheapest channel.
- Pair with a labour-condition badge alongside the existing carbon-savings badge at point of transaction, reusing the same UI mechanism and the same behavioural rationale (Peleg Mizrachi et al.'s per-transaction disclosure finding, already cited in the KaPhor paper for the carbon badge).
## **4.2 Gap 12: No system distinguishes domestic vs. imported waste routing despite materially different downstream risk**
Imported waste is a minority of India's total textile waste (7% nationally) but is concentrated in specific high-volume, high-risk hubs like Panipat, where worker-health outcomes are most severely documented. A platform that is agnostic to this distinction may inadvertently route volume toward the highest-risk part of the system.
### **Proposed KaPhor component: Source-Aware Routing Preference**
- Add a data field to each partner facility record: domestic-sourced-only vs. mixed/import-exposed input stream.
- Allow users to set a routing preference (similar to a “local-only” sourcing filter in food delivery apps), defaulting to domestic-sourced partners where available.
- Use this field internally to weight the LOE's recycling allocation toward verified, domestically-focused partners as the partner network grows, without requiring the user to take any extra action.

## **4.4 Connection to existing Future Scope**
The KaPhor paper's Future Scope (Section VII) already proposes training and certifying independent tailors and upcyclers as verified service providers (Future Scope point 5). The Verified Facility Tier proposed here is structurally identical — a verification and disclosure layer applied to a different node in the same supply chain. These two could be unified into a single future-scope direction: a Verified Partner Network covering both repair/upcycling labour and recycling-facility labour standards under one certification framework, which would also strengthen the paper's existing claim about localised job generation by extending it explicitly to job quality, not just job creation.


# **5. Caveats and Open Data Gaps**
In the interest of giving an accurate picture rather than a clean one, the following limitations in the underlying research should be carried into any paper or product decision built on this document:

- No source provides a verified, government-published count of textile recycling centers in India. All facility-level figures in this document (e.g. Panipat's unit counts) come from journalism and industry blogs, not regulatory filings, and figures from different sources for the same metric (e.g. Panipat's unit count: ~100 vs. ~20,000) disagree by two orders of magnitude, likely due to different definitions of what counts as a “unit.”
- Total annual waste-generation estimates range from 1.2 million to 8 million tonnes depending on source; the 7.7–7.8 million tonnes figure is used here because it is corroborated by three independent sources (Fashion for Good, CSTEP, Landbell), but this is a judgement call, not a settled number.
- Textile-specific EPR registration data (parallel to the plastic/e-waste/battery EPR counts that do exist) was not located in this research — if it exists, it would substantially improve the precision of any “number of formal recycling entities” claim.
- Consumer-facing drop-off networks (Goonj, SADS, etc.) publish city-level coverage but not exact addresses or coordinates for most locations; a small number of physical addresses were found for Delhi-NCR and Mumbai operations specifically (see prior research thread), but this is not representative national coverage.
- The worker-health findings are well-corroborated across five independent outlets but are specific to Panipat; whether the same severity applies to other, less-documented recycling clusters in India is not established by any source reviewed.
